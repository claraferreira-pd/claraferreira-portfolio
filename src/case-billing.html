My role

I was the design lead, working with product, engineering and the billing team from the first discovery session to the specs engineering built from. The flows, the visual language and the decisions are mine.

The interactive prototype was built with AI, from my designs and direction.

The problem

Billing ran on Stripe’s built-in subscription logic. It could charge a card, but every failed payment and every plan that didn’t fit its rules turned into manual work for the billing team.

Stripe reports. The platform decides.

Stripe keeps the charge and the stored card. Everything else, from plans to dunning, moved into the platform.

How I built it

  1. 01 Discovery

    Short working sessions with the billing team to map what they did by hand and what could run on its own.

  2. 02 Prototype

    Reviewed a working console with the team early, so their feedback shaped the flows before handoff.

  3. 03 Rules

    Turned each decision into a business rule, like how many payment methods an account can hold and in what order they’re charged.

  4. 04 Specs

    Wrote screen-by-screen specs with acceptance criteria, so engineering built exactly what was agreed.

Two problems worth solving

Out of everything the billing team handled by hand, two problems mattered most to the business.
01

One card failing shouldn’t fail the payment

Accounts can now keep up to three payment methods: a default and two backups, by card or bank account. When a charge fails, the next method is tried automatically, before the account ever reaches dunning.

−84% accounts in dunning

02

Plans built from parts

The business was expanding past its current plans, and the old setup couldn’t take a new service without rebuilding billing around it. In the new hub, plans, add-ons and coupons are separate building blocks, so a new service becomes a new plan instead of a new project.

Reusing what customers already know

To change a plan on someone’s behalf, the billing team uses the same builder customers see. The total updates with every choice, and Confirm stays off until something actually changes.

When every method fails

The account enters a recovery sequence the team configures: reminders and retries, with a timeline of what happened and what comes next. Fewer accounts land here now, and the ones that do don’t need a support ticket.

A way out before cancelling

Cancelling starts with a choice: leave, or move to a lighter Maintenance plan that keeps the account alive.

Still usable on a phone

Tables become cards and tabs scroll instead of wrapping. Same console, real breakpoints.

Small rules that kept it consistent

  1. Buttons

    One primary action per view, labeled with exactly what it does: “Confirm subscription change”, never “Submit”.

  2. Status

    Color only ever means status. A badge is never colored for emphasis, so an account and its invoices always agree.

  3. Proration

    The unused-time credit and the new charge appear before the total, because a total on its own invites a support ticket.
